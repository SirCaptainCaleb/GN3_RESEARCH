# Adjacent chamber certificates glue precisely through a sign-agreement class

For a binary sign word epsilon=(epsilon_1,...,epsilon_m), use distinct increasing positions t_i and define the certificate polytope K(epsilon) by lambda_i>=0, sum lambda_i=1, sum lambda_i epsilon_i=0, and sum lambda_i epsilon_i t_i=0. The affine threshold theorem shows that K is nonempty exactly when epsilon has at least two changes.

Proposition 1. The vertices of K are precisely the three-point certificates on alternating triples i<j<k with epsilon_i=epsilon_k=-epsilon_j. No certificate can have support one or two: for opposite signs the zeroth moment forces equal mass, and the first moment would force equal positions. A vertex has support at most three, because with more than three positive coordinates the three linear equations admit a nonzero supported perturbation, in both directions while preserving nonnegativity. On a three-element support the two equal signs must bracket the opposite sign, and the coefficients are the unique positive interpolation weights of Subsection 97. Conversely that unique solution on its support is a vertex. Hence K is the convex hull of its alternating-triple certificates.

Proposition 2: shared-certificate criterion. For two words epsilon and eta of the same length, let D={i:eta_i=-epsilon_i} and E={i:eta_i=epsilon_i}. Then K(epsilon) intersect K(eta) is nonempty if and only if the restriction of epsilon to D or to E has at least two changes. Indeed adding and subtracting the moment equations shows that a common certificate has zero signed zeroth and first moments separately on D and E. At least one class has positive total mass; normalization then gives a certificate on that class. Conversely any certificate supported in D or E is common, since all signs in that class either reverse together or remain unchanged. In particular every nonempty intersection contains a shared three-point certificate.

More precisely, the intersection is the convex hull of the two embedded certificate polytopes on D and E, omitting empty ones. Thus an admissible interface can be filled using positive combinations of genuine certificates from one sign-agreement class. There is no need to invent mixed-order window data at such an interface.

Example showing that some interfaces are absent. The words 01000 and 00010 are both bad, with signs interpreted as (-1)^bit. Here D={2,4}; the restricted first word is 10. On E={1,3,5} it is 000. Neither has two changes, so their certificate polytopes are disjoint. This pair can occur on adjacent coordinate chambers for reversal-odd ternary data: take orders (1,2,3,4,5,6,7) and (1,2,4,3,5,6,7) and prescribe their five windows to be these words. Their only common window is (5,6,7), colored 0 in both; no two other prescribed triples belong to the same reversal orbit. Assign the reverse of each prescribed triple the opposite color and complete the other reversal orbits arbitrarily. This is a realizability example for the interface failure, not a NOR counterexample.

For a swap of adjacent coordinates in general coordinate arity r, the differing window positions lie in an interval of at most r+1 positions. Therefore any alternating triple outside that interval supplies a shared certificate. This supplies a concrete rule for gluing certificates where possible and identifies the interfaces a modified complex must address. Merely subdividing an interface does not by itself provide a certificate common to its two original endpoint words.
