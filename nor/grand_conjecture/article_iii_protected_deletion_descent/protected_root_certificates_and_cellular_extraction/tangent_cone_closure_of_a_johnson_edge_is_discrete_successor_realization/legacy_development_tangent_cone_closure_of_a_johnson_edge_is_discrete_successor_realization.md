## Tangent-cone closure of a Johnson edge is discrete successor realization

Let R be a nonempty finite family of p-subsets of V, with 0<p<|V|, and put
\[
P=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf1_D:D\in R\}.
\]
Centering the vectors makes no difference to the following statements. Fix C in R, a in C and c outside C, and let
\[
C^+=(C\setminus\{a\})\cup\{c\},\qquad d=e_c-e_a.
\]

### Theorem

The following are equivalent:

1. d belongs to the feasible tangent cone T_P(1_C).
2. 1_C+eta d belongs to P for some eta>0.
3. C^+ belongs to R.

Moreover if C^+ is absent, there is an explicit integer linear potential with coordinate weights in {-2,0,1,2} uniquely maximized on R at C, while C^+ has strictly larger potential.

Thus the tangent-cone premise in root §63 is not a weaker convex surrogate for successor realization. At these hypersimplex vertices it is precisely the discrete realization premise.

### Proof

For a polytope the tangent cone at a vertex is generated by its vertex displacement vectors; a direction in it can be followed for a sufficiently short positive distance inside P. This gives 1=>2. Statement 3 gives 2 with eta=1 and hence 1.

To prove 2=>3, write
\[
\mathbf1_C+\eta(e_c-e_a)=\sum_{D\in R}\lambda_D\mathbf1_D,
\quad \lambda_D\ge0,\quad\sum_D\lambda_D=1.
\]
Coordinates other than a,c remain exactly their original values 0 or 1. At a coordinate whose average is 0, every positive-weight vertex has that coordinate 0; at a coordinate whose average is 1, every positive-weight vertex has it 1. Therefore every D with lambda_D>0 agrees with C outside {a,c}. Since |D|=p, it is either C or C^+. The c-coordinate on the left is eta>0, so C^+ must occur with positive weight and belongs to R.

This is a face statement: the coordinate constraints fixing V minus {a,c} cut the ambient hypersimplex down to its Johnson edge [1_C,1_C^+]. The attained-cut convex hull meets this edge away from C only if its other endpoint is attained.

### Explicit exposure of a missing successor

Put K=C minus {a} and U=V minus (C union {c}). Define
\[
w_v=
\begin{cases}
2,&v\in K,\\
0,&v=a,\\
1,&v=c,\\
-2,&v\in U.
\end{cases}
\]
Then Phi(D)=sum_{v in D} w_v satisfies
\[
\Phi(C)=2(p-1),\qquad\Phi(C^+)=2(p-1)+1.
\]
Every p-set D other than C,C^+ either misses a member of K or includes a member of U. Hence
\[
\Phi(D)=2|D\cap K|+\mathbf1_{c\in D}-2|D\cap U|
\le2(p-1)-1.
\]
If C^+ is absent from R, C is the unique global maximizer of Phi on R, separated from all other attained shores by a gap of at least one; the forced successor gains exactly one.

This exposure does not require zero-freeness of any root family. It applies to every missing Johnson successor.

### Application and boundary of the reduction

For root §63, checking tangent-cone closure at every realized shore is exactly checking that every chosen successor is realized. Under that premise, the finite successor graph already has a directed shore cycle. A continuous Brouwer interpolation proves no weaker attainability statement that bypasses this discrete premise.

The explicit bounded-integer potential can replace an arbitrary supporting functional when studying a single missing edge. Its role is to retain one concrete extremal witness during a repair analysis; it does not prove that a threshold repair realizes the successor.

A cycle of shore edges should not automatically be called a simple physical root cycle or assigned the one-token normal form. Those are distinct objects; the focused audit following this lemma states the distinction.
