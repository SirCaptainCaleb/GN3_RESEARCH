# R191 — No order-ten smallest boundary-tournament counterexample

## Statement
There is no order-ten smallest counterexample to the Strong Level-(1) two-cover conjecture.

## Hypotheses
Standing project definitions; accepted R4, R8, R128, R183 and R184.

## Scope
Parent closure of the entire order-ten smallest-counterexample case. The only remaining minimum-overlap branch is m=1, which is excluded by structural four-core clique counting.

## Applicability
Use as the order-ten closure theorem. Any global reduction whose terminal hypothetical counterexample has order ten may consume this directly.

## Persistence
Global contradiction in the standing order-ten smallest-counterexample setting.

## Exclusions / nonclaims
Does not assert that every hypothetical smallest counterexample has order ten; that is a separate global reduction if available.

## Proof

Assume for contradiction that H is an order-ten smallest Strong Level-(1) counterexample. Let F be the family of all five-element vertex sets that support a tight Hamilton P5. We deliberately do not define F using global-longest paths.

1. Clean support-family geometry.

First, union F=V(H). Fix v in V(H) and choose any six-set E containing v. By R195, at least four of the six five-subsets E-{e} support a P5. Only one of those six subsets omits v, namely E-{v}; hence at least one good five-subset contains v.

Second, intersection F is empty. Fix v. Since H has ten vertices, choose a six-set E contained in V(H)-{v}. R195 supplies a P5 support inside E, hence a member of F avoiding v.

Third, any two members of F intersect. If A,B in F were disjoint, then because |A|=|B|=5 and |H|=10 they would be complementary. Choosing a tight Hamilton P5 on A and one on B would give a spanning two-cover of H, contradiction.

Finally, some two members meet in at most three vertices. Suppose instead that every two distinct members of F meet in at least four vertices. Since union F has ten vertices and every member has size five, F has two distinct members A,B. Their intersection has size exactly four; write C=A cap B, A=C union {a}, B=C union {b}. Consider any D in F. If C is contained in D, call D star-type. Otherwise D misses some c in C. It cannot miss two vertices of C, because then |D cap A|<=3. Thus it misses exactly one c. The inequalities |D cap A|>=4 and |D cap B|>=4 force A-{c} and B-{c} both into D, and their union already has five vertices. Hence D=(C-{c}) union {a,b}, a top-type member contained in A union B. If any top-type member exists, then a star-type member C union {d} with d outside {a,b} would meet it in only the three vertices C-{c}. Therefore the only star-type members are A and B, and every member of F is contained in A union B, contradicting union F=V(H). If no top-type member exists, every member contains C, contradicting intersection F=empty. Hence, writing m for the minimum intersection of two distinct members of F, we have 1<=m<=3.

2. Minimum intersection three is impossible.

Suppose A,B in F have minimum intersection S=A cap B of size three. By R8 orient S as a tight trimer. Minimality R4 gives pc(H-S)<=2. If H-S were Hamiltonian, a Hamilton path on H-S together with the tight trimer on S would two-cover H, impossible. Thus H-S has an exact two-cover P|Q.

If one rail has order five, it itself is a member of F disjoint from S. If one rail has order six, R195 applied to its six-vertex support supplies a five-vertex Hamilton support D contained in that rail and disjoint from S. Either case contradicts minimum intersection three, since A-S has only two vertices and therefore |D cap A|<=2. Consequently both rails have order at most four. Since they cover seven vertices, their profile is 4+3. The three-rail is a tight trimer and the four-rail is a disjoint tight P4, so R126 forces a tight P5 contained in H-S, giving the same contradiction. Thus m is not three.

3. Minimum intersection two is impossible.

Suppose A,B in F have minimum intersection S of size two. Put U=A-S, V=B-S, and R=V(H)-(A union B), so |U|=|V|=3 and |R|=2. Apply clean Pair-Deletion Rigidity R429 to S. It gives an exact two-cover P|Q of H-S and both rails are nontrivial. If a rail has order five it is a P5 support; if a rail has order six, R195 supplies a P5 support inside it. If both rails have order at most four, then because they are nontrivial and cover eight vertices they have profile 4+4; taking a consecutive tight trimer from one rail and the other P4, R126 again supplies a P5. Therefore in every case there is D in F with D cap S empty.

Minimum intersection two with A and B forces |D cap U|>=2 and |D cap V|>=2. Since |D|=5, the profile of D across U,V,R is one of (2,2,1), (2,3,0), (3,2,0). Choose L from {A,B} so that |L cap D|=2, and let M be the other one. Put X=L cap M=S, Y=L cap D, and Z=M cap D. Then X and Y are disjoint two-subsets of L. Any vertex of Z cap L would belong to both X and Y, so Z is disjoint from L. Minimum intersection gives |Z|>=2, while Z is contained in M-L, a three-set, so |Z| is two or three.

If |Z|=2, write
L=X union Y union {p},
M=X union Z union {b},
D=Y union Z union {d},
and let e be the unique vertex outside L union M union D. Orient P0={p,b,e} as a tight trimer by R8. This trimer is disjoint from D. Write X={x1,x2} and choose any r in D. R146 applied to P0 and the three exterior vertices x1,x2,r forces a P5 on one of P0 union {x1,x2}, P0 union {x1,r}, P0 union {x2,r}. Each candidate meets D in at most one vertex. It is therefore a member of F intersecting D in fewer than two vertices, contradicting minimality of m.

If |Z|=3, write
L=X union Y union {p},
M=X union Z,
D=Y union Z,
and let e,f be the two vertices outside L union M union D. Orient P0={p,e,f} as a tight trimer. For any distinct u,v in X union Y union Z, the five-set P0 union {u,v} cannot support a P5. Indeed, if it did, minimum intersection two with both M and D would force u,v to lie in M cap D=Z, because P0 is disjoint from M and D. But then this P5 support would meet L only in p, contradicting m=2. Now choose any three distinct u,v,w from the seven-set X union Y union Z. R146 applied to P0 and u,v,w forces one of the three pair-extensions to support a P5, contradiction. Hence m is not two.

4. Minimum intersection one is impossible.

Suppose m=1. Fix A in F and let E=V(H)-A. The five-set E is not in F, because otherwise the P5s on A and E would be a spanning two-cover. Fix a in A. For z in E put B_z=(E-{z}) union {a}. We claim at most one of the five B_z fails to lie in F. If two distinct B_x,B_y were bad, put P0=E-{x,y}, a three-set, and orient P0 as a tight trimer using R8. R146 applied to P0 and the exterior vertices x,y,a forces one of
E=P0 union {x,y},
B_y=P0 union {x,a},
B_x=P0 union {y,a}
to support a P5, contradicting the assumed badness of all three. Thus for each a in A at least four of the five B_z belong to F. As a ranges over A, these give at least twenty distinct members of F meeting A in exactly one vertex.

Let barF={V(H)-B : B in F}. The families F and barF are disjoint, since a set lying in both would be complementary to another member of F and the two corresponding P5s would span H. Work in the Johnson graph J(10,5). For every successful replacement B_z, its complement is C_{a,z}=(A-{a}) union {z}, which lies in barF and is a Johnson neighbor of A. The twenty distinct successful pairs (a,z) therefore give at least twenty F-to-barF Johnson edges incident with every A in F. Summing from the F side gives
 e_J(F,barF) >= 20|F|.

For the opposite bound, for every four-set C let K_C={C union {v}: v notin C}; this is a K6 in J(10,5), and every Johnson edge belongs to exactly one such clique, indexed by the four-set intersection of its endpoints. Put r_C=|F cap K_C| and s_C=|barF cap K_C|. Since F and barF are disjoint, r_C+s_C<=6. Hence
 r_C s_C <= (r_C+s_C)^2/4 <= (3/2)(r_C+s_C).
Summing over all four-sets C counts every F-to-barF Johnson edge exactly once. Each five-set contains five four-subsets, so sum_C r_C=5|F| and sum_C s_C=5|barF|=5|F|. Therefore
 e_J(F,barF) <= (3/2)(10|F|)=15|F|,
contradicting the lower bound 20|F|.

All possible minimum intersections m=1,2,3 are impossible. This contradiction proves that no order-ten smallest Strong Level-(1) counterexample exists, exactly the conclusion of R191.

The proof uses only current clean R4, R8, R126, R146, R195, and R429. It does not use R9, R10-R15, R24, R5, R127-R131, R141, R147, R168, R169, R183, R184, R189, R190, or any singleton-order-floor descendant. The historical P191 route is preserved separately.
