## Research note

**Theorem 1 (corank/dual-basis sweep).** The two canonical models have full permutation galleries whose colored exchange states are in bijection with one basis of the dual matroid.

For \(M=\bigoplus_{i=1}^kU_{1,2}\), choose a dual basis \(D\) and its complementary primal basis \(B=E(M)\setminus D\). Given any permutation \(d_1,\ldots,d_k\) of \(D\), successively replace in \(B\) the element parallel to \(d_i\) by \(d_i\). This is a basis-graph antipodal geodesic with \(k=r(M^*)\) exchange edges, distinctly labeled by \(D\). For \(M=U_{2,n}\), any dual basis \(D\) has complementary primal basis \(\{s,t\}\). Given a permutation \(d_1,\ldots,d_{n-2}\) of \(D\), take the consecutive two-element windows of the injective word \((s,d_1,\ldots,d_{n-2},t)\). Their successive exchanges are \((s,d_1,d_2),\ldots,(d_{n-3},d_{n-2},t)\), with one distinct retained middle element \(d_i\) per exchange. They number \(n-2=r(M^*)\), and conversely every Hamiltonian tight word with endpoints \(s,t\) arises in this manner. The cube's exchange label is its inserted dual-basis element; the boundary exchange label is its retained dual-basis element. Applying cube complementation after path reversal, or reversing the boundary word, induces \(w\mapsto\overline{\operatorname{rev}w}\) on the color word.

*Proof.* A dual basis is the complement of a primal basis. In the partition matroid, all selected exchanges lie in distinct two-element parallel classes, so successive toggles are legal, and after all toggles the endpoint is complementary. In \(U_{2,n}\), consecutive unordered pairs are bases, each adjacent pair of bases intersects in the middle word element, and the interior word symbols form \(D\). The stated counts, converses, and reversal identities follow immediately. \(\square\)

**Theorem 2 (classification of the three-circuit class).** Every connected component of a finite loopless matroid in which all circuits have size at most three has rank at most two. Its simplification is a direct sum of simple uniform rank-two components and coloops.

*Proof.* In a simple matroid all nontrivial circuits are triangles. Suppose two triangles \(C_1=\{e,a,b\}\) and \(C_2=\{e,c,d\}\) lie in different rank-two flats. Their union has rank three. Every three-subset of \(\{a,b,c,d\}\) contains two points of one triangle and a third point outside its rank-two flat, and is independent. The four-subset is dependent, because its rank is at most three; it is therefore a four-element circuit, contrary to hypothesis. Thus any two intersecting triangles lie in the same rank-two flat. Matroid connected components are generated by overlapping circuits, so every nontrivial simple connected component lies in a rank-two flat and is a rank-two uniform matroid. Parallel-element deletion gives the simplification without increasing circuit cardinalities, and restoring parallel classes cannot increase the rank of a component. \(\square\)

**Theorem 3 (no canonical basis polarity for uniform rank two).** For \(n\ge5\), any permutation of the bases of \(U_{2,n}\) commuting with all matroid automorphisms is the identity.

*Proof.* Its automorphism group is \(S_n\). The setwise stabilizer of \(\{1,2\}\) is \(S_2\times S_{n-2}\), and for \(n\ge5\) fixes no other two-element subset. An equivariant basis map must therefore fix \(\{1,2\}\), hence by transitivity fixes every basis. In particular there is no free equivariant involution on the unordered bases. For \(\bigoplus U_{1,2}\), by contrast, toggling every parallel pair gives a free involution commuting with the full automorphism group. The tournament reversal acts canonically on *oriented exchange edges* \((a,b,c)\leftrightarrow(c,b,a)\), rather than on bases. \(\square\)

**Theorem 4 (ordinary orientation cannot carry every boundary coloring).** An orientation of \(U_{2,n}\) on its original ground set cannot by itself encode all reversal-odd boundary 3-tournaments for \(n\ge5\).

*Proof.* The \(\frac12 n(n-1)(n-2)\) reversal orbits of ordered distinct triples can be colored independently, giving \(2^{n(n-1)(n-2)/2}\) boundary colorings. Every simple oriented matroid of rank two is realizable by nonparallel nonzero planar vectors. The circular order of their \(2n\) labeled signed rays determines all its chirotope signs, so there are at most \((2n)!\) such labeled oriented matroids. For \(n=5\), \(2^{30}>10!\); the inequality grows stronger for larger \(n\). An injective encoding is impossible. \(\square\)

**Proposition 5 (parallel-class obstruction to corank bounds).** Let \(M_m\) be the connected rank-two matroid having three parallel classes of cardinalities \(m,1,1\). Its circuits have size two or three and its dual rank is \(m\). Yet every injective original-element word \((v_1,\ldots,v_\ell)\) in which each consecutive pair is a basis has \(\ell\le5\): no two entries of the \(m\)-element parallel class can be consecutive, while only two distinct entries are available outside that class. Thus a polynomial-in-corank lower bound for vertex-simple sliding-pair galleries fails even in the uncolored three-circuit class. Simplicity, or a comparable bound on parallel-class concentration, is necessary.

**Interpretation.** The shared primal-matroid data are small fundamental circuits and dual-basis permutations; the common resource budget is corank. A common proof requires additional *state-level* direction, polarity, and ordered-resource constraints. Theorems 3–4 rule out a free basis-level involution on uniform rank two and encoding arbitrary boundary colorings by an orientation of that original rank-two matroid. They leave open an intrinsically defined, canonically oriented exchange-state geometry. Theorem 1 is a possible starting point for a common resource-rank augmentation conjecture, while Proposition 5 rules out extending its quantitative conclusion to all loopless small-circuit matroids.
