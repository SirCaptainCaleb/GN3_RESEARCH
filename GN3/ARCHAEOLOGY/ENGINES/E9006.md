# E9006: Bounded terminal-wall phase-zero entrance compressor

## Theorem

Let `H` be a hypothetical smallest counterexample to the two-path-cover theorem in the class of finite Strong Level-(1) boundary tournaments, and suppose

`n=|H|>10`.

Assume that we are given a certified E9003 terminal-wall phase-zero entrance: a literal no-slide spanning three-path forest

`A|B|C`,

a retained tight turn of the marked wall rail `A`, and the wall-born physically disjoint opposite-polarity signed pair supplied by the terminal-wall construction. Thus the birth has profile `2+2` when `|A|>=4`, and profile `1+2` when `|A|=3`.

Then there is a finite ancestry-preserving continuation with one of the following outcomes:

1. `H` has a spanning cover by two tight paths; or
2. there is a fixed physical pair `E={a,c}` and a graph-intrinsic nonquiet signed-anchor contact arising from a retained selected-incidence episode on an exact frame

   `H-E=U|V`,

   namely strict tight-path growth, a vertex-simple proper tight cycle, or explicit reverse-contact/reversal geometry in the sense of Spare Part S9010, *Signed-Path Contact and Historical Anchor Protection*.

The touching object in the second outcome is historical graph-intrinsic certificate data arising from the selected-incidence episode; it need not remain selected in the exact `H-E` representative used at the episode.

Moreover, after the first both-singleton floor is reached and its physical pair is declared to be `E`, at most two selected-incidence episodes can return quietly to the same `E`. The next selected-incidence episode is necessarily nonquiet. Thus the post-floor continuation has the bounded macro form

`emptyset -> {a} -> {a,c} -> nonquiet`,

with a two-cover or nonquiet exit allowed at every arrow.

The proof uses only boundary antisymmetry, smallest-counterexample minimality, and the published Spare Parts S9010 and S9014, *Signed-Interval Rebirth and Tight-Path Cut Memory*.

## 1. Pair-deletion rigidity

For any distinct vertices `p,t`, the subsystem `H-{p,t}` has path-cover number exactly two, and both rails of every exact two-cover are nontrivial.

Indeed, minimality gives a cover of `H-{p,t}` by at most two tight paths. If the residue had a Hamilton path

`W=(w_0,w_1,...)`,

then boundary antisymmetry on the three vertices `{p,w_0,t}` would give a tight trimer on those vertices. That trimer together with the suffix `(w_1,...)` would two-cover `H`, contrary to the choice of `H`.

Likewise, suppose an exact two-cover of `H-{p,t}` had a singleton rail `{u}`. Boundary antisymmetry on `{p,u,t}` gives a tight trimer on those three vertices, and that trimer together with the other rail would again two-cover `H`. Hence every exact pair-deletion two-cover has two nontrivial rails.

We will use this fact repeatedly without further comment.

## 2. Paying the terminal-wall pair to a genuine floor

We first show that every wall-born pair can be reduced, without losing its ancestry, to two singleton supports unless `H` closes earlier.

### Lemma 2.1 — finite pair descent

Any wall-born opposite-polarity pair of profile `2+2`, `1+2`, or `1+3` has a finite certificate-retaining continuation to a spanning two-cover or to an ancestry-bearing pair of two singleton supports.

### Proof

Begin with the singleton/nontrivial case. Write the fixed singleton as `(p)` and orient the other signed support as

`T=(t_0,...,t_m)`, `m>=1`,

so that `t_0` is its unsigned end. Cover the outside reservoir by at most two tight paths. Whenever one outside component begins with a selected state `u_0u_1`, boundary antisymmetry on `(t_0,u_0,u_1)` gives one of

`(t_0,u_0,u_1)`, `(u_1,u_0,t_0)`

tight. This trimer meets the pair only at `t_0`; deleting that contact leaves the suffix

`(t_1,...,t_m)`

signed by S9014 and leaves `(p)` untouched. Thus every nonclosing repetition lowers `|T|` by one. If the outside reservoir consists only of singleton components, the corresponding dimer or one-vertex seam test gives the same one-end refund. Therefore the process reaches a singleton on the `T` side unless a spanning two-cover appears first.

It remains to treat width two. More generally, suppose one member of a balanced pair is the signed dimer

`P=(p_0,p_1)`

and the opposite support `T` is nontrivial. Cover the outside reservoir by at most two paths and choose a nonempty component

`U=(u_0,...,u_k)`.

If `U` is nontrivial, the only new seams in the proposed concatenation `(P,U,T)` are

`(p_0,p_1,u_0)`,
`(p_1,u_0,u_1)`,
`(u_{k-1},u_k,t_0)`,
`(u_k,t_0,t_1)`,

with absent endpoint seams omitted. If `U` is a singleton, the analogous three seams are obtained by identifying its two boundary roles. If every displayed seam is tight, the concatenation together with the unused outside rail is a spanning two-cover.

Suppose some displayed seam is bad. Its complete reversal is a tight trimer. Except for the two boundary swallowing cases considered below, that reverse trimer removes only an unsigned end of one support, or one unsigned end from each support, and S9014 leaves a strictly smaller signed descendant pair.

For the first swallowing case, suppose the initial seam `(p_0,p_1,u_0)` is bad. Then

`(u_0,p_1,p_0)`

is tight and meets all of `P`. Write the head sign of `P` as

`(alpha,p_0,p_1)`

after reversing the polarity convention if necessary. Choose the outside head `u_0` distinct from `alpha`; unless this can be done, the existing signed extension together with the opposite support already yields a spanning two-cover. Boundary antisymmetry on the distinct triple `{u_0,p_0,alpha}` gives one of

`(u_0,p_0,alpha)`, `(alpha,p_0,u_0)`

tight. This second marker meets `P` only at `p_0`, so after the contact S9014 leaves the singleton `(p_1)` as a signed descendant.

There is one symmetric swallowing case. If the opposite support is also a dimer,

`T=(t_0,t_1)`,

and the final seam `(u_k,t_0,t_1)` is bad, then its reversal

`(t_1,t_0,u_k)`

swallows all of `T`. Exchange the roles of `P` and `T` and take the head/tail dual of the preceding repair: choose the relevant outside boundary vertex distinct from the sign witness of `T` unless the existing signed extension already closes to a two-cover, apply boundary antisymmetry to the corresponding second-marker triple, and use S9014 to retain the opposite singleton descendant.

Thus, up to exchanging the two supports and taking the head/tail dual, every width-two swallowing event reduces to the first repair above. Consequently every nonclosing width-two step produces a descendant balanced pair with a singleton support, after which the singleton/nontrivial argument finishes the descent.

This proves the lemma. ∎

Applying Lemma 2.1 to the certified E9003 wall birth therefore gives either a spanning two-cover or a first ancestry-bearing both-singleton floor.

## 3. Prescribed replacement of one floor coordinate

The fixed-endpoint argument will need to move one singleton coordinate while retaining the other.

### Lemma 3.1 — prescribed floor-coordinate replacement

Suppose an ancestry-bearing floor has singleton supports `(p),(t)`, and let `x` be distinct from `p,t`. Then there is a finite certificate-retaining continuation to a two-cover or to the floor `(p),(x)`, with `p` fixed throughout.

### Proof

By pair-deletion rigidity choose an exact two-cover

`H-{p,t}=U|V`

with both rails nontrivial. The vertex `x` lies on one rail, say `U`, and hence lies on a selected adjacency dimer. Orient that dimer as `(d,x)`; both orders of a dimer are tight paths.

Regard `(p)` as head-signed by `t`, since the two-vertex word `(t,p)` is automatically tight. Test the turn `(d,x,t)`. If it is tight, `(d,x)` is an opposite tail-signed support with signed endpoint `x`. If it is bad, boundary antisymmetry gives `(t,x,d)` tight, so `(t,x)` is an opposite tail-signed dimer, again with signed endpoint `x`.

In either case `(p)` is untouched and we have a singleton/dimer opposite-polarity pair whose dimer has unsigned first vertex and signed endpoint `x`. Apply the singleton/nontrivial descent from Lemma 2.1, always peeling the unsigned end. S9014 preserves the endpoint `x`; unless a two-cover occurs, the result is exactly the floor `(p),(x)`.

The head/tail dual replaces the other coordinate while retaining `t`. ∎

## 4. Selected-incidence extraction

Fix a both-singleton floor

`E={a,c}`

and an exact frame

`H-E=U|V`.

Call a vertex `b` an **admissible middle** if it is internal to one of the two displayed rails. For an admissible middle `b`, boundary antisymmetry lets us orient the pair `E` so that

`J_b=(a,b,c)`

is tight. Let

`r,b,s`

be the predecessor-middle-successor segment of the rail containing `b`.

### Lemma 4.1 — endpoint-anchored dimer extraction

The predecessor incidence at `b` produces a nontrivial signed dimer whose signed endpoint is `a`; dually the successor incidence produces one whose signed endpoint is `c`.

### Proof

Boundary antisymmetry on `{r,a,b}` gives exactly one of

`(r,a,b)`, `(b,a,r)`

tight. In the first case `(r,a)` is tail-signed by `b`; in the second `(b,a)` is tail-signed by `r`. In either case the signed endpoint is the physical vertex `a`.

Dually, boundary antisymmetry on `{b,c,s}` gives either `(b,c,s)` or `(s,c,b)` tight, producing a head-signed dimer anchored at `c`. ∎

Every such anchored dimer can be completed to a genuine opposite-polarity pair without losing its identity. For example, let `D` be tail-signed by witness `w`. Delete `V(D)` and apply pair-deletion rigidity to obtain an exact two-cover

`X|Y`

with both rails nontrivial. Exactly one rail contains `w`; let the other be

`M=(m_0,m_1,...)`.

If `(w,m_0,m_1)` is tight, then `M` is head-signed by `w`. If it is bad, boundary antisymmetry gives `(m_1,m_0,w)` tight, so `(m_0,w)` is head-signed by `m_1`. Either way we obtain a nonempty head-signed support disjoint from `D`, hence a balanced opposite-polarity pair retaining the anchored dimer `D`. The head-signed case is dual.

## 5. Protected completion of a fresh endpoint

### Lemma 5.1 — protected endpoint completion

Suppose an endpoint-anchored dimer `D_e` has just been produced at a floor on `E={a,c}`, where `e` is one of `a,c`, and suppose no earlier completed selected-incidence episode has retained `(e)` as a signed singleton at an `E`-floor. Then there is a finite chosen continuation yielding one of:

1. a spanning two-cover;
2. an explicit S9010 contact/growth/cycle/reversal output involving the protected anchor `e`; or
3. another floor on the same physical pair `E`, now retaining `(e)` as a historical signed singleton certificate.

### Proof

Complete `D_e` to a balanced opposite-polarity pair as above and designate its signed endpoint `e` as protected. Descend the pair by the finite seam tests of Lemma 2.1.

Whenever both supports have order at least three, sandwich an outside-cover component between them. A bad seam reverses to a tight trimer and strictly shortens one or both signed supports; S9014 rebirths the surviving signed interval. At width two use the complete boundary-swallowing analysis from Lemma 2.1.

If the support containing `e` is ever met at `e` itself, the touching tight path is a genuinely later nontrivial path containing the old signed anchor. S9010 then gives outcome 2, and the quiet branch terminates. Otherwise S9010 and S9014 retain a nonempty signed descendant with the same anchor `e`.

On every quiet step total pair mass strictly decreases. Hence either the `e`-support becomes the singleton `(e)`, or the opposite support becomes singleton first. In the latter case fix that singleton and run the singleton/nontrivial descent on the `e`-support. A quiet descent can only peel vertices away from `e`, so it eventually reaches `(e)`. Once `(e)` is singleton, keep it fixed and pay the opposite support to singleton width.

Thus, unless outcomes 1 or 2 occurred, we reach a floor

`(e),(q)`

for some vertex `q`. Let `f` be the other member of `E`. If `q=f`, we are already aligned. Otherwise apply Lemma 3.1 with `(e)` fixed and target `f`. This yields a two-cover or the floor `(e),(f)`, retaining the completed singleton certificate `(e)`. ∎

## 6. The middle-independent endpoint budget

At a fixed physical floor `E={a,c}`, define the **completed-endpoint ledger**

`A_E subseteq E`

as follows. An endpoint `e` belongs to `A_E` exactly when an earlier selected-incidence episode on this same physical pair returned quietly to an `E`-floor and retained `(e)` as a graph-intrinsic signed singleton certificate.

This ledger records physical endpoint ancestry. It does not record the middle vertex or the particular pair-deletion representative used during the episode.

### Lemma 6.1 — fresh-or-spent dichotomy

At any later `E`-floor and any admissible middle, deliberately testing the channel anchored at `e in E` has exactly the following alternatives:

- a spanning two-cover occurs;
- an explicit S9010 nonquiet contact/growth/cycle/reversal occurs; or
- `e` was fresh, and a quiet protected continuation returns to an `E`-floor with `e` adjoined to `A_E`.

In particular, if `e in A_E`, the third alternative is impossible.

### Proof

Lemma 4.1 produces a new nontrivial signed dimer `D_e` anchored at `e`. If `e notin A_E`, Lemma 5.1 gives precisely the displayed trichotomy, and its quiet branch returns with `(e)` retained. Thus `e` enters `A_E`.

Suppose instead that `e in A_E`. Retain the historical signed singleton

`P=(e)`

from the earlier completed episode. The new dimer `D_e` is a genuinely later proper tight path containing the signed anchor of `P`; it is not the vacuous exact replay of the singleton. By S9010, one obtains strict growth of the old support or of the new path, a proper tight cycle, or explicit reverse-contact/reversal geometry. Thus a spent endpoint can never complete another quiet episode. ∎

The same argument proves **middle independence**. Changing from one admissible middle to another changes neither the physical pair `E` nor any historical singleton certificate defining `A_E`. The new middle merely supplies a new selected predecessor/successor and hence, by Lemma 4.1, another dimer at whichever physical endpoint is tested. Therefore Lemma 6.1 remains valid after arbitrary rebasing of the middle.

### Lemma 6.2 — an admissible middle always exists

At every `E`-floor, pair-deletion rigidity gives an exact two-cover

`H-E=U|V`

with `|U|,|V|>=2`. The total number of vertices internal to the two displayed rails is

`(|U|-2)+(|V|-2)=n-6`.

Since `n>10`, this number is at least five. Hence an admissible middle exists at every return to `E`.

For any such middle `b`, exactly one of `(a,b,c)` and `(c,b,a)` is tight. In the first orientation the predecessor channel is anchored at `a` and the successor channel at `c`; in the second orientation the roles are reversed. Thus either physical endpoint of `E` can be deliberately tested at every admissible middle. ∎

## 7. Completion of the Engine

Apply Lemma 2.1 to the certified terminal-wall birth. Unless `H` is already two-covered, the continuation reaches a first ancestry-bearing both-singleton floor. Declare the unordered physical singleton pair at this first floor to be

`E={a,c}`.

At the moment of declaration no selected-incidence episode on this fixed `E` has yet completed. Therefore

`A_E=emptyset`.

The original terminal-wall rail, its retained turn, and the wall-birth certificate remain historical graph-intrinsic ancestry; the endpoint-budget argument does not require them to remain selected in later representatives.

Choose any exact frame `H-E=U|V` and any admissible internal middle. By Lemma 6.2 both endpoint channels are available. Test the channel anchored at `a`. Lemma 6.1 gives a two-cover, an explicit nonquiet S9010 output, or a quiet return to an `E`-floor with

`A_E={a}`.

Only the third case remains. At the returned floor choose any exact pair-deletion frame and any admissible middle; no relation to the previous middle is required. Test the channel anchored at `c`. Again Lemma 6.1 gives a two-cover, an explicit nonquiet output, or a quiet return with

`A_E={a,c}=E`.

Only the last case remains. Rebase once more to any admissible middle and test either endpoint channel. That endpoint is already in `A_E`, so the spent-endpoint clause of Lemma 6.1 applies immediately. The newly extracted nontrivial anchored dimer meets the retained historical singleton at its signed anchor, and S9010 yields explicit strict path growth, a vertex-simple proper tight cycle, or reverse-contact/reversal geometry. A third quiet return is impossible.

Thus every nonclosing continuation from the terminal-wall entrance has the finite macro pattern

`A_E=emptyset -> {a} -> E -> nonquiet`,

where either of the first two arrows may instead terminate immediately in a two-cover or a nonquiet output. The proof therefore requires at most two quiet endpoint-completion episodes after the first floor and one final selected-incidence test. No repeated payment-return cycle, representative-novelty argument, or unbounded descent clock is needed.

For the nonquiet outcome retain the following data:

- the physical endpoint pair `E`;
- the exact pair-deletion frame used at the terminal selected-incidence episode;
- its chosen internal middle and selected predecessor/successor incidence;
- the completed-endpoint ledger accumulated before that episode;
- the historical singleton certificate at the spent endpoint;
- the newly extracted anchored dimer and its sign witness; and
- the specific S9010 output.

These data are graph-intrinsic certificates and constitute the exported E9006 portal. A later touching marker need not remain selected in the retained exact frame; the interface is historical rather than a simultaneous-currentness assertion.

The Engine deliberately stops at this portal. A proper cycle, strict signed growth, or reverse contact is an explicit nonquiet geometric interface, not asserted here to be a contradiction by itself. Consuming that portal belongs downstream.

This proves E9006. ∎
