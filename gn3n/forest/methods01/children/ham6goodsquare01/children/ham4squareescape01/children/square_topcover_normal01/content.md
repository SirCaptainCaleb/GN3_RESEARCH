# A two-label path-cover square has a coherent endpoint cover, a permanently internal label, or top-state cover disagreement

## Statement

Let H be a minimum counterexample, let D be a Hamiltonian four-set, let d,e be distinct vertices outside D, and put K=H-(D union {d,e}). Assume K, K+d, K+e, and K+d+e are non-Hamiltonian with path-cover number two. Consider the family of all two-covers of G=K+d+e. Then at least one of the following holds. (1) Some two-cover F of G has both d and e as displayed endpoints; consequently F, F-d, F-e, and F-{d,e} are two-covers of the four square states, giving a coherent endpoint square. (2) One of d,e is internal in every two-cover of G. (3) There are two two-covers of G with different support partitions. (4) There are two Hamilton paths on one common component support of two covers of G whose vertex orders disagree. In particular the generic component-drop crossing alternative can be replaced by the much sharper permanent-internal/disagreement dichotomy unless a coherent endpoint square already exists.

## Body

If some two-cover F has both d and e as endpoints, deleting either endpoint preserves a tight path on its surviving component. No component can be exactly {d} or {e}, because that would make the complementary lower square state Hamiltonian; and no component can be exactly {d,e}, because K is non-Hamiltonian. Hence deleting d, e, or both leaves two nonempty tight paths and gives outcome (1). Now assume no top cover has both labels as endpoints. Then every top cover has d internal or e internal. If d is internal in every top cover, outcome (2) holds, and likewise for e. Otherwise choose F with d an endpoint, so e is internal, and choose F prime with e an endpoint, so d is internal. If their unordered support partitions differ, outcome (3) holds. If the support partitions agree, let A be the common support class containing d. The F-component on A is a Hamilton path with d at an endpoint, while the F-prime component on the same support A is a Hamilton path with d internal. Their linear orders cannot be identical, so some pair of common vertices occurs in opposite relative order; this is outcome (4).
