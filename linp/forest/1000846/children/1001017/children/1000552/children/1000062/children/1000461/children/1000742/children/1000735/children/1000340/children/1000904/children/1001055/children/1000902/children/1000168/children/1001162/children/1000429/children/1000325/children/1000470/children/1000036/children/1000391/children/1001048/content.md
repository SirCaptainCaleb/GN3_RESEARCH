# An all-top edge forces a multiple-overlap pair among its three maximum endpoint paths

## Statement

Let L be the global maximum linear-path length and let h={a,b,c} be an edge with phi(a)=phi(b)=phi(c)=L. Choose arbitrary maximum L-edge endpoint paths P_a,P_b,P_c ending physically at a,b,c respectively. Then every pair among P_a,P_b,P_c intersects. Moreover, at least one pair has at least two common vertices. More precisely, if P_a and P_b have a unique common vertex, that vertex must be c and is a same-index internal joint on both paths; cyclically for the other pairs.

## Body

First fix two endpoints, say a and b. If P_a avoided both b and c, then h is not an edge of P_a and h meets V(P_a) only at the physical endpoint a. Appending h through a would therefore give an (L+1)-edge linear path, contradicting global maximality of L. Hence P_a contains b or c. Similarly P_b contains a or c. If P_a contains b then P_a meets P_b at b; if P_b contains a then they meet at a; otherwise both contain c. Thus P_a and P_b intersect. The same argument applies cyclically. Now suppose P_a and P_b have exactly one common vertex. By 5854d853a44b that vertex is an internal joint on both paths. It cannot be a, since a is the physical endpoint of P_a, and cannot be b, since b is the physical endpoint of P_b. Hence the unique common vertex is c, at the same joint index on both paths. Cyclically, if all three path pairs had unique intersections, then P_a intersect P_b={c}, P_b intersect P_c={a}, and P_c intersect P_a={b}. By 5854d853a44b there are indices alpha,beta,gamma such that c occurs at index alpha on P_a,P_b, a at index beta on P_b,P_c, and b at index gamma on P_c,P_a. The vertices a,b,c are distinct, so alpha,beta,gamma are distinct. Choose two of the three indices whose distance is smallest; the corresponding two intersection vertices lie on one of the three host paths, and the host segment between them has length equal to that smallest distance. The alternative route through the third intersection vertex, using the other two maximum paths, has length equal to the sum of the other two index distances and is strictly longer. Because the three path pairs have only their designated common vertices, the alternative route is internally vertex-disjoint from the replaced host segment and from the rest of the host path. Replacing the host segment by this longer two-path route therefore gives a linear path ending at the same physical endpoint but longer than L, contradiction. Thus some pair has at least two common vertices.
