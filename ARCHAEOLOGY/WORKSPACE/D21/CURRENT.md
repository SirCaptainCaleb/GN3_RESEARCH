## Exact puncture accounting and prescribed-path completion

For a partition of a selected path cover into vertex classes, let `pc(X)` denote the number of path components induced in a class and let `t` count selected transitions between classes. The exact identity developed in R7 and reused by R20/R21 is

`t = Σ_X pc(X) - k + σ`,

where `k` is the number of selected cover rails and `σ≥0` is the appropriate cycle/excess term. The proof is elementary but fundamental: split every selected rail at every inter-class transition, count the resulting monochromatic path blocks, and compare the number of blocks with the number of original rails. R20 specializes the count to deletion of a prescribed carrier: surviving historical runs are the color classes, so every extra fragmentation beyond the number of rails forces an additional carrier/exterior transition. R21 combines this with exact completion of a proper prescribed path. In the nonclosing branch the complement is non-Hamiltonian, hence has at least two path components, and the punctured representative must select the crossing states needed by the deficit.

R17 packages the same bookkeeping as **gain minus cycle debt**: if a local move introduces raw gain `g` selected states but closes `c` tight cycles, the usable path-cover gain is `g-c`. R19 is the universal internal-vertex graft instance. These identities are graph/cover exact and remain useful independently of the later historical terminology. R6,R16,R18,R22,R23 are early parent/specialized formulations of this same puncture-completion program; their durable content is the exact deficit and prescribed-path completion mechanism, while later developments give cleaner canonical interfaces.



## Transfer edges, fixed shadows, root basins, and endcap saturation

R25-R34 build the first transfer language. The common mechanism is: a puncture crossing is not merely an edge between historical pieces. At a selected carrier boundary it can be compared with the old adjacent carrier state; failure of a literal splice forces the complete-reversal alternative by boundary antisymmetry. Repeating the comparison at both ends produces transfer edges, same-rail endpoint absorption, central absorption packets, a fixed common shadow, signed attachment sides, root basins, and endcap saturation.

The useful invariant is physical and local. Once a physical support `{u,v}` and a witness `w` are fixed, boundary antisymmetry certifies exactly one of the two complete-reversal turns with the prescribed middle. What may be retained across later representatives is the resulting graph-intrinsic tight turn and any explicitly preserved historical event. What may *not* be silently retained is the old selected orientation, old rail identity, old residual rails, or endpoint status. R27-R34 should therefore be read as the historical derivation of this support calculus, not as permission to transport an entire cover frame.

R30-R34 additionally show why extremal transfer components are useful: bi-end locking forces consistent attachment-side data; root basins isolate where transfer can re-enter; and attachment-fiber/endcap saturation turns repeated local failures into a bounded collection of physical supports. These ideas are superseded operationally by later signed-support theorems but preserve the origin of the geometry.



## Global-longest, root, pivot, and seam precursors to payment

R43-R63 are the historical bridge from local support facts to payment. Global-longest endpoint gaps, outer blockers, universal root reverse turns, pivot grids, triple-root shadows, root rotations, reverse-excess signatures, internal-return concentration, seam selection, and one-/two-seam reductions manufacture a physical signed support plus a controlled seam on which the generic D16 refund/payment step can run. Several later arguments no longer need these extremal wrappers, but these specialized constructions retain stronger physical labels than the generic payment theorem and therefore remain distinct.
